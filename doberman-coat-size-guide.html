How to Measure a Doberman for a Winter Jacket or Raincoat: Coat Size Guide

Three measurements, the right chart and one simple rule for deep-chested dogs, with a checker that picks the size for you.

A Doberman coat size guide starts with one honest admission: the coat you hoped would fit straight off the shelf probably won't. Dobermans are tall, deep-chested and narrow at the waist, so most charts built for "average" dogs leave the chest pinched, the belly bare or the back half-covered. Three numbers taken with a soft tape, read against the brand's own chart, fix most of that.

Quick answer: Stand your Doberman square and measure three things in inches and centimeters: back length (from the base of the neck, where the collar sits, to the base of the tail), chest girth (around the widest part of the ribcage, just behind the front legs) and neck girth (where the collar sits). Choose the size whose chest range covers your dog first, then check the back length. If your dog sits between two sizes, take the larger one for an insulated winter jacket and look for an adjustable belly strap to close the gap at the narrow waist. A raincoat can sit closer to the body.

Enter your dog's numbers and up to three rows from the size chart of the coat you are looking at. The checker converts units, flags measuring mistakes and tells you which size fits best, and why.

Why are Dobermans so hard to fit for a coat?

Because the breed combines a deep, wide chest with a tucked-up waist and a tall, square frame, and most coat charts assume a fuller, longer, more tube-shaped body. The fix is to fit the chest first and treat the waist as something you adjust, not something you size for.

The FCI Dobermann breed standard describes exactly the shape that makes shopping awkward. The chest depth should be about 50% of the dog's height at the withers, with a well-developed forechest. From the breastbone back to the pelvis the underline is "noticeably tucked up". The body looks almost square: in males, the length from the tip of the breast to the point of the buttock should be no more than 5% longer than the height at the withers, and no more than 10% in females.

In this short clip from Pet Circle, a veterinarian shows where the tape goes for each coat measurement, and why a coat that is too long rubs under the front legs while one that is too short does little.

How big is a Doberman, by the standard?

The FCI standard gives a height at the withers of 68–72 cm for males and 63–68 cm for females, with weights of about 40–45 kg and 32–35 kg. In US units that is roughly 26.8–28.3 in and 24.8–26.8 in at the shoulder, and about 88–99 lb and 71–77 lb. Your own dog may sit above or below these ranges, especially in American lines or in a still-growing young dog. That is exactly why weight-based coat charts fail: two 80 lb Dobermans can need different sizes.

Why the chest is the hard part

In an interview on Vetstreet about fitting dog coats, Angela Cuce of Cool Blue Dog Apparel names the chest (girth) as "by far" the most difficult part to fit. She explains that many coats close under the neck and under the belly and leave the chest exposed, even though the chest and underbelly need the most cover in cold, wet weather. She also points out that most charts guide by weight and length, which only works for dogs with "perfect" proportions. A Doberman is not built like the average chart dog.

Does a Doberman really need a winter coat?

Many do, on cold or wet walks, because the breed has a short, tight coat with no undercoat to trap warm air. A coat is not a fashion choice for this breed in winter; it is the layer nature left out.

The FCI standard describes the Dobermann's hair as short, hard and dense, lying tight and smooth, and states that undercoat is not allowed. Compare that with the thick double coats of cold-weather breeds. The UK veterinary charity PDSA, in its vet Q&A on winter coats, says dogs with fine or thin fur, and dogs that are very young, underweight, old or unwell, feel the cold much faster and need a warm coat for walks, as well as shorter outings. It adds that most dogs don't need a coat indoors, because they can overheat in centrally heated homes.

Signs your dog is telling you something

  • Shivering or slowing down on a walk. PDSA's advice is to bring the dog home and into the warmth straight away.
  • Reluctance to leave the porch on a wet, windy morning.
  • Tucking the tail and hunching the back when standing still outside.

If your Doberman burns energy hard every day, a good coat keeps that routine going through the cold months instead of cutting walks short. Our guide to how much exercise a Doberman needs covers what a full day looks like.

How do you measure a Doberman for a coat?

Take three measurements with a soft tape while your dog stands square on a flat floor: back length, chest girth and neck girth. Write each one down in both inches and centimeters, because coat charts use either.

PDSA's guide uses the same three: length from the neck (collar) to the base of the tail, chest just behind the front legs, and the neck where the collar normally sits. Its tip is to keep the numbers in both centimeters and inches.

1. Back length

Find the base of the neck, the spot where a flat collar rests just in front of the shoulder blades. Run the tape along the spine to the point where the tail joins the body. Keep the tape flat along the back, not floating above it. This is the number most brands print first on their chart.

2. Chest girth

Wrap the tape around the deepest, widest part of the ribcage, just behind the front legs. On a Doberman this is well forward, because the chest is deep and the waist tucks up quickly behind it. Don't measure around the belly: it is much narrower and changes with meals and breathing. Pull the tape snug, not tight. You should be able to slide two flat fingers under it.

3. Neck girth

Measure around the base of the neck, where the collar sits, not high up behind the ears. A Doberman's neck is long and muscular, and the base is noticeably thicker than the top. A collar opening sized to the top of the neck will choke at the base.

Stand, don't sit

A sitting dog curves its spine and shortens its back measurement. Canine Journal's guide to DIY dog clothing notes a difference of 1 to 2 inches when a dog's back is measured sitting versus standing. Measure standing, twice, and use the larger of the two readings if they differ.

A dog that can stand calmly for a minute makes all of this much easier. If yours treats the tape as a toy, practice a short settle first. Our guide to the Doberman off switch and calm settling shows how to build that pause.

Winter jacket vs raincoat: does the size change?

The measurements stay the same, but the margin changes. An insulated winter jacket needs a little room for loft and for a thin layer underneath, while a raincoat or shell can sit closer, as long as your dog can still stride and squat freely.

Adjustable straps are your friend

The Vetstreet interview describes a "belly belt" with hook-and-loop closure on the underside of the jacket, which cinches the coat for a smaller waistline. Cuce says it adds 1 to 2 inches of adjustment on the underside. For a Doberman's deep chest and narrow waist, that kind of strap closes exactly the gap a fixed coat can't.

What if your Doberman falls between two sizes?

Let the chest decide. If the chest is above the smaller size's maximum, go up, then handle any extra back length with straps or a short alteration. A coat that is too tight across the chest is the one fit problem you can't adjust away.

This is where most owners feel stuck, and where it helps to accept that there is no perfect off-the-rack match for every dog. The Vetstreet interview gives a useful reference point: on a typical chart, a 32-inch chest pairs with a length of about 22 to 23 inches, sometimes more. If your dog's chest is large for its back length, that mismatch is normal for a deep-chested breed, not a sign you measured badly.

Three common between-size cases

  • Chest fits the smaller size, back fits the larger. Go larger for a winter jacket. The extra chest room is welcome under padding, and a belly strap takes up the slack.
  • Chest fits the larger size, back fits the smaller. Go larger and check that the hem doesn't hang past the base of the tail or rub under the front legs. Cuce notes that going up a size for the chest may mean altering the length.
  • Neck is the only problem. Look for a coat with an adjustable or wide neck opening rather than changing size, because the body fit matters more.

How should a Doberman coat fit when it's on?

It should cover the chest, the back to the base of the tail and as much of the belly as the design allows, without limiting your dog's stride, squatting or turning. If your dog keeps biting, scratching or rubbing at the coat, the fit is wrong.

PDSA says a dog should be able to move freely in its coat and do everything it usually does without it, including running, jumping, peeing and pooping. Biting or scratching at the coat, or rubbing against furniture or the floor, is a sign it doesn't fit and irritates the dog. The Vetstreet interview makes the same practical point: check that the garment leaves room to relieve itself comfortably, for both males and females.

The two-minute fit test

  1. Put the coat on and fasten every strap snug, not tight.
  2. Slide two fingers under the neck opening and under the chest strap. Both should fit flat.
  3. Watch your dog walk, trot and turn. Look for rubbing behind the elbows and under the front legs.
  4. Ask for a sit and a down. The coat shouldn't pull hard across the shoulders or ride up to the neck.
  5. Check the back hem: it should end near the base of the tail, not halfway along the back.
  6. Take a short real walk. Then check the skin under the straps and behind the front legs for red marks.

Step-by-step: from tape measure to the right coat

Here is the full routine as a checklist. It takes about ten minutes, plus the time spent reading the chart properly.

  1. Pick your moment. Measure after a walk, when your dog is calm and willing to stand.
  2. Use a soft sewing tape. A rigid metal tape won't follow the chest or spine. A piece of string and a ruler works if you have nothing else.
  3. Stand your dog square on a flat, non-slip floor, head level, weight on all four feet.
  4. Measure back length from the collar base to the base of the tail. Twice.
  5. Measure chest girth at the deepest part, just behind the front legs, two fingers under the tape.
  6. Measure neck girth at the base of the neck.
  7. Write all three down in inches and centimeters (1 inch = 2.54 cm).
  8. Open the specific coat's chart, not the brand's general chart. Sizes differ between models from the same maker.
  9. Fit the chest first, then check back length, then the neck.
  10. Run the numbers through the checker at the top of this page.
  11. Check the return policy before you buy, so a size swap is easy.
  12. Do the fit test above on the first wear, indoors, before a real walk.

If your dog doesn't enjoy being handled, build up slowly with treats, the same way you would for grooming. Our guide to trimming Doberman nails uses the same small-steps approach: touch, treat, stop while your dog is still relaxed.

When the old coat no longer fits

A coat that fit last winter may not fit this one, and that is normal. Re-measure every season, and more often for puppies and older dogs, whose bodies change the most.

Growing puppies

A Doberman puppy can outgrow a coat within weeks. Buy adjustable, inexpensive layers for the first winter, and re-measure every few weeks rather than buying a size "to grow into" that rubs and slips. If you are planning that first year, our Doberman puppy schedule by age shows how the daily routine, including walks, changes month by month.

Senior dogs

Older Dobermans often lose muscle along the back and thighs. The coat that once hugged a powerful frame can slip and twist. It can be hard to notice that change in a dog you love, and harder to accept it. PDSA lists old and unwell dogs among those that feel the cold much faster, so the right coat matters more, not less, in the later years. Re-measure, choose a softer, lighter jacket if the old one feels heavy, and keep walks shorter in the cold. Letting go of the old size is part of looking after the dog in front of you now.

Common mistakes when sizing a Doberman coat

Most sizing problems come from measuring the wrong spot, trusting weight charts, or buying for the waist instead of the chest.

  • Ordering by weight or breed name. A "Doberman size" label tells you little. Two dogs of the same weight can need different coats.
  • Measuring the belly instead of the chest. On a tucked-up breed, this can understate the girth by several inches.
  • Measuring while the dog sits. The back reads 1 to 2 inches short, per Canine Journal.
  • Measuring the neck behind the ears. The base of a Doberman's neck is much thicker.
  • Using the brand's general chart. Each model can be cut differently.
  • Choosing the smaller size to avoid a loose waist. A tight chest restricts movement; a loose waist can be strapped.
  • A coat that closes only under the neck and belly. Vetstreet's expert notes these leave the chest exposed, which is the part that needs cover most.
  • Leaving the coat on indoors. PDSA warns that dogs can overheat in heated homes.
  • Never re-checking. Puppies grow, adults gain or lose muscle, and seniors change shape.

Doberman coat sizing FAQ

What size coat does a Doberman wear?

There is no single Doberman size. Many adults land in a brand's large or extra-large range, but only your dog's chest girth, back length and neck girth, compared with the specific coat's chart, tell you the right size.

Which measurement matters most for a Doberman coat?

Chest girth. A Doberman's deep chest is the hardest part to fit, so choose the size whose chest range covers your dog, then check back length and neck.

Where do I measure back length on a Doberman?

From the base of the neck, where the collar sits, along the spine to the base of the tail, with your dog standing square. Measuring while sitting can read 1 to 2 inches short.

Should I size up for a Doberman winter jacket?

If your dog falls between two sizes, usually yes. An insulated jacket needs a little room for padding, and an adjustable belly strap can tighten the narrow waist.

Do Dobermans need a coat in winter?

Many do on cold or wet walks. The breed standard describes a short, tight coat with no undercoat, and PDSA advises warm coats for dogs with thin fur and for young, old or unwell dogs.

How do I know if my Doberman's coat fits?

Your dog should move, sit, lie down, pee and poop normally in it. Two fingers should slide under the neck and chest straps. Biting, scratching or rubbing at the coat means the fit is wrong.

How often should I re-measure my Doberman?

Every season for adults, every few weeks for growing puppies, and whenever a senior dog's coat starts slipping or twisting.

Summary: measure the dog, not the breed

A good Doberman coat size guide comes down to three numbers and one rule. Measure back length, chest girth and neck girth with your dog standing, keep them in inches and centimeters, and let the chest decide between sizes. Pick a design that covers the chest and belly, use straps to deal with the narrow waist, and test the fit before the first cold walk. When the coat stops fitting, as it will for puppies and seniors, re-measure and move on to the size your dog needs now.
