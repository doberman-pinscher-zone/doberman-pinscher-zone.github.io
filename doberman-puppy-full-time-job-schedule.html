Raising a Doberman Puppy With a Full-Time Job: Hour-by-Hour Schedules From 8 Weeks to 12 Months

Age-by-age day plans for working owners, from the first week home to the first birthday.

Raising a Doberman puppy with a full-time job works when you plan the day around one number: the longest stretch your puppy can wait for a bathroom break at its current age. Picture coming home at six to a 10-month-old Doberman who stretches, yawns and walks out of the crate to greet you, instead of a dog who has chewed the baseboards. That calm dog is built hour by hour, and the hours change every month.

Quick answer: Under 10 weeks, a puppy can't be alone for more than about 1 hour, so plan time off or have someone home. At 10–12 weeks, plan for about 2 hours between breaks. From 3 to 6 months, add about 1 hour per month of age (3 hours at 3 months, 6 hours at 6 months). Past 6 months, the AKC's ceiling is 6–8 hours. For a typical 9–10 hour workday plus commute, that means 3–4 midday visits at 10–12 weeks, 2 at 4 months and 1 from about 5 months on, even for a dog older than 6 months.

How long can a Doberman puppy be left alone at each age?

Use the AKC's age chart: about 1 hour under 10 weeks, 2 hours at 10–12 weeks, then roughly 1 hour per month of age up to 6 hours at 6 months. Even adult dogs shouldn't go much past 6–8 hours without a bathroom break.

The AKC's guide to leaving a puppy alone lists these limits: under 10 weeks, 1 hour; 10–12 weeks, 2 hours; 3 months, 3 hours; 4 months, 4 hours; 5 months, 5 hours; 6 months, 6 hours; over 6 months, a maximum of 6–8 hours. The same guide says that even adult dogs shouldn't be home alone for much longer than six to eight hours without a chance for a bathroom break.

Treat each number as a ceiling, not a target. A puppy that has just eaten, woken up or played needs to go much sooner. Plan your breaks so the longest gap is a little under the limit, not right on it.

Why this matters more with a Doberman

The Dobermann was bred in Thuringia, Germany, in about 1890 by a tax collector, Louis Dobermann, as Wikipedia's breed history explains. It was a working breed from the start. A bored, uncomfortable Doberman puppy does not just whine. It looks for a job, and an empty kitchen offers plenty of bad ones. A schedule that covers bathroom breaks, short play and real rest prevents most of the trouble before it starts.

What does a full-time owner's day look like at 8 to 12 weeks?

At 8–10 weeks, a normal office day doesn't work without help, because the puppy can only manage about an hour alone. At 10–12 weeks, plan a break about every 2 hours, which means 3–4 visits during a 9–10 hour absence.

The AKC suggests taking vacation time when you first bring a puppy home. The first week or two is the cheapest moment to be home: the puppy is learning where to toilet, where to sleep and that the crate is safe. If you can't take time off, line up a family member, neighbor or paid sitter before pickup day, not after the first accident.

Sample day for a 10–12 week Doberman puppy

Example: out of the door at 7:45, home at 17:30 (9 hours 45 minutes away).

  • 6:15 Wake up, straight outside to toilet. Calm praise.
  • 6:30 Breakfast, then outside again.
  • 6:45–7:15 Play, a little exploring in the yard, two or three 2-minute training games (name, sit, hand touch).
  • 7:15–7:35 Quiet time in the crate or pen while you get ready, so being shut in is not linked only with you leaving.
  • 7:40 Last toilet trip, into the crate with a safe chew. Leave without a fuss.
  • 9:40, 11:40, 13:35, 15:35 Visits: toilet, 10–15 minutes of play and sniffing, back in with a chew.
  • 17:30 You're home. Toilet first, greeting second.
  • 17:45–20:30 Dinner, short training games, handling practice, naps between activities, toilet trips after each.
  • 21:30–22:30 Last toilet trip and bedtime in the crate near your bed.

Young puppies sleep a large part of the day. That is good news for working owners, because the time between your visits should mostly be nap time. A puppy who has had a toilet break and some play before each quiet stretch settles much faster than one who is put away full of energy.

Crate, pen or dog walker: what do working owners actually use?

Most working owners combine a crate for the alone stretches with a person who comes in at midday. Experienced Doberman owners are often wary of daycare for young pups and lean toward a trusted walker or sitter.

In a July 2026 r/DobermanPinscher thread on raising a puppy with a full-time job, a couple in Toronto asked how other owners managed the first 6–12 months. The answers covered almost every real option: one owner came home every day at lunch for about an hour, another had a dog sitter come in for walks every day for the first couple of years, one worked from home, and one ended up quitting the job. The top comment suggested that first-time owners look at adopting an older Doberman, because Doberman puppies are a lot of work.

The crate vs pen question

A 2022 thread on Doberman Chat, "First time Doberman owner and working full time", came from a new owner who would be away from about 9:30 to 7:30. Replies warned that an indoor pen lets a puppy soil its own area, which slows house training. One owner said a pen works well, but only for the first few weeks, because the puppies grow fast. Another wrote that 10 hours is too long to leave a pup crated or penned. The original poster decided to wait until he had a stable schedule and could pay for regular sitters.

What a good midday visit includes

  • Straight outside to the same toilet spot.
  • 10–20 minutes of play, sniffing or a short walk suited to the puppy's age.
  • One or two 2-minute training games, using the same cue words you use.
  • Back into the crate with a fresh chew, calmly.
  • A short note or text: toilet done, anything chewed, how settled the puppy was.

How do you teach a Doberman puppy to be alone before your first workday?

Start while you're still at home: short absences of seconds and minutes, rewarded with calm, and grown in small steps. By the time you go back to work, being alone should be familiar, not a shock.

The AKC recommends crate training while you're home and then leaving the puppy alone for longer stretches once it's comfortable. In the video below, Kikopup, a professional trainer, shows a step-by-step separation training plan for puppies and adult dogs, ending with real alone time.

A simple alone-time ladder

  1. Puppy in the crate with a chew while you sit in the same room.
  2. You walk around the room, then step out of sight for a few seconds.
  3. Out of the room for 1 minute, then 5, then 15, while the puppy chews or naps.
  4. Out of the house for a short trip: take out the trash, check the mail.
  5. A 30–60 minute errand, then a "fake workday" morning with your real leaving routine.

If the puppy cries hard each time, go back a step and make the next one smaller. A calm settle on a mat helps here too. Our guide to teaching a Doberman an off switch walks through rewarding relaxation in timed steps, and it pairs well with alone-time practice.

How much exercise fits around a workday?

For a puppy, short and frequent beats long and rare. Split activity into small blocks before work, at each visit and in the evening, and put naps between them.

A widely used guideline for puppies is about 5 minutes of structured exercise per month of age, twice a day, as our Doberman exercise guide explains along with adult targets. Free play, sniffing and training games come on top of that. Don't try to "tire out" a young Doberman with a long run before work. An over-tired puppy is often harder to settle, not easier.

Mental work counts

Ten minutes of training, a food puzzle or a sniff walk can do as much for calm as a longer walk. Feeding part of the breakfast in a puzzle toy or a stuffed toy in the crate gives your puppy something to do in the first quiet minutes after you leave.

Step-by-step: set up your workday plan before pickup day

  1. Time your real day. Write down when you leave and when you get back, door to door. Most people find it is 9–11 hours, not 8.
  2. Run the numbers. Use the planner above to see how many visits each month needs.
  3. Book the people first. Arrange time off for the first days, then line up visitors for the first 3 months, when you need the most visits.
  4. Set up the space. A crate sized for a growing Doberman, a spot near family life, safe chews, and nothing chewable within reach.
  5. Write a one-page routine. Toilet spot, cue words, chews allowed, crate rules. Give a copy to every visitor.
  6. Practice alone time. Start the alone-time ladder on day one, in tiny steps.
  7. Review every month. As your puppy grows, drop a visit only when it has been clean and calm for the longer stretch.

Mistakes that make the first months harder

  • Planning around an 8-hour day that is really 10. The commute counts. Measure your real absence.
  • Dropping visits too early. The AKC chart is a ceiling. If your 4-month-old has accidents at the 4-hour mark, keep 3 visits a little longer.
  • Leaving straight after high energy. Toilet, play, then a calm 10 minutes, then leave. A buzzing puppy fights the crate.
  • Making leaving and coming home a big event. Keep both quiet so the door is not a trigger.
  • Using a pen that the puppy has outgrown. Owners on Doberman Chat found pens work only for the first few weeks. A fast-growing puppy needs a safe, secure setup.
  • Different rules from every visitor. Give everyone the same one-page routine.

Doberman puppy and full-time job FAQ

Can I get a Doberman puppy if I work full time?

Yes, if you can cover the breaks. Under 10 weeks you need someone at home. From 10–12 weeks you need a break about every 2 hours, then fewer visits as the puppy grows. Many owners use a lunch visit, a sitter or a family member, and some choose an older dog instead.

At what age can a Doberman be left alone for a full workday?

No age makes a 9–10 hour absence without a break ideal. The AKC says even adult dogs shouldn't be home alone much longer than six to eight hours without a bathroom break, so plan at least one midday visit for long days.

How many times a day does a dog walker need to come for a 3-month-old puppy?

At 3 months the AKC limit is about 3 hours. For a 9 hour 45 minute absence, that is 3 visits, spaced about every 2.5 hours.

Is a crate or a playpen better for a working owner's Doberman puppy?

Many experienced owners prefer a crate for the alone stretches, because a pen with space to soil can slow house training. A pen can work for the first few weeks, but Doberman puppies outgrow them quickly.

Should I take time off when I bring my Doberman puppy home?

If you can, yes. The AKC suggests taking vacation time when you first bring a puppy home, so the puppy settles in and you can start crate training and alone-time practice.

Is doggy daycare a good option for a young Doberman?

Many Doberman owners on Reddit advise against daycare for young puppies and prefer a trusted walker or sitter. Some daycares also won't accept very young puppies.

Summary: your Doberman puppy schedule, month by month

A Doberman puppy and a full-time job can work well together when the schedule follows your puppy's age. Under 10 weeks, someone stays home. From 10–12 weeks, plan breaks every 2 hours; at 3 months, every 3 hours; from 5 months, one midday visit covers most office days. Keep that visit through the first year. Add short training, quiet crate time and gradual alone-time practice, and by 12 months you'll have the dog you pictured: one that rests while you work and is ready to go when you walk through the door.
